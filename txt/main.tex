\section{Introduction}

(more reference)
Prime editing represents a cutting-edge gene editing technology that enables precise and flexible modifications of the genome on across multiple species, including human\cite{davisEfficientPrimeEditing2024}. 

While CRISPR-Cas9 disrupts the genome with double strand breaks, and base editing accurately targets single base changes, prime editing is a more flexible 'genomic word processor' that can perform all possible edit types, including substitutions, insertions and deletions\cite{koeppelPredictionPrimeEditing2023}.


\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{diagrams/illustration/prime editing process.pdf}
    \caption{Prime editing components and mechanism~\cite{anzaloneSearchandreplaceGenomeEditing2019}.
    A prime editor (PE) fuses a Cas9 nickase with reverse transcriptase; both protein modules are omitted here for visual clarity.
    The prime editing guide RNA (pegRNA) contains a target-directing \textit{spacer}, a Cas9 \textit{scaffold}, a \textit{primer-binding site} (PBS), and a \textit{reverse-transcriptase template} (RTT) encoding the desired change as left homology arm (LHA), \textit{edit}, and right homology arm (RHA); the \textit{PAM} (red) enables target recognition and nicking.}
    \label{fig:pe-process}
\end{figure}


Prime editing installs programmed sequence changes through a nick-and-copy mechanism~\cite{anzaloneSearchandreplaceGenomeEditing2019}.
After the prime editor--pegRNA complex binds the target locus and nicks the PAM-containing strand, the exposed 3$'$ end hybridizes to the pegRNA and primes reverse transcription of the desired edit into the genome (\autoref{fig:pe-process}, steps~1--3).
The steps that follow are less deterministic.
Reverse transcription generates a branched intermediate in which the newly synthesized edited 3$'$ flap and the displaced original 5$'$ flap can equilibrate between competing annealing configurations (step~4).
Cellular flap-processing enzymes such as FEN1 preferentially excise the 5$'$ flap and ligate the edited strand when flap resolution favours the edit (step~5), but this excision step does not always work in the editor's favour: the edited flap may fail to invade the duplex, or 3$'$ exonucleases may degrade it instead, restoring the original sequence and leaving the target available for another round of editing from step~1~\cite{anzaloneSearchandreplaceGenomeEditing2019}.
Even when the edited strand is ligated in, the resulting heteroduplex still contains mismatches between the edited and unedited strands that must be resolved by mismatch repair (MMR) or replication (step~6).
Because MMR can copy from either strand, it may reject the edit and rebuild the original allele and reset the locus~\cite{anzaloneSearchandreplaceGenomeEditing2019,chenEnhancedPrimeEditing2021}.
These competing repair outcomes are a major reason why raw prime editing efficiencies are often modest, and motivate enhanced systems such as PE3~\cite{anzaloneSearchandreplaceGenomeEditing2019}, which nicks the non-edited strand to bias heteroduplex resolution, and PE4/PE5, which transiently suppress MMR with a dominant-negative MLH1 protein~\cite{chenEnhancedPrimeEditing2021}.

On top of the competing repair outcomes, editing efficiency is also heavily shaped by target context and pegRNA design.
Cis-chromatin position effects and local epigenetic marks can strongly modulate prime editing even for a fixed pegRNA~\cite{liChromatinContextdependentRegulation2024,mathisMachineLearningPrediction2024}.
Because these locus-level effects are largely separable from pegRNA sequence design and are typically modelled as a separate problem (e.g.\ ePRIDICT), chromatin context is not the focus of this study.
Instead, this work centres on sequence-level determinants of efficiency.
High-throughput analyses show that position-specific nucleotide composition in the target and pegRNA flanking regions---including PBS and reverse-transcriptase template (RTT) GC content, PAM choice, and the identity of the last templated nucleotide---can shift activity by orders of magnitude~\cite{kimPredictingEfficiencyPrime2021,kimChromatinStructureContextdependent2023}.
pegRNA architecture adds another layer of control: optimal primer-binding site (PBS) length depends on delivery format and on melting temperature of PBS--target hybridization, because complementarity between the PBS and spacer can auto-inhibit target binding~\cite{ponnienselvanReducingInherentAutoinhibitory2023}, and degradation of the 3$'$ RTT--PBS extension can poison editing unless the pegRNA is stabilized, for example with engineered 3$'$ RNA structures~\cite{nelsonEngineeredPegRNAsImprove2022}.
Nevertheless, many sequence determinants remain only partly understood.
For some edit classes, such as insertions, it remains unclear which sequence and repair features govern efficiency even after large-scale profiling~\cite{koeppelPredictionPrimeEditing2023}.
As a result, efficient prime editing often requires laborious empirical optimization rather than design from first principles~\cite{kimChromatinStructureContextdependent2023,domanDesigningExecutingPrime2022,hsuMechanisticMachineLearning2026}.
The large number of possible pegRNA designs---varying spacer location, PBS length, and RTT length~\cite{hsuMechanisticMachineLearning2026}, as well as silent edits that can improve efficiency~\cite{chenEnhancedPrimeEditing2021}---further limits the practicality of designing prime editors for real-world applications by hand.


This motivates computational tools for in silico pegRNA design given a target sequence and a desired edit.
Such tools generally operate in two stages.
First, candidate pegRNAs are proposed from physical constraints such as PAM availability and limits on PBS and RTT length; most pipelines follow similar principles here.
The second, more consequential stage ranks those candidates with a model trained on experimental efficiency data.
Three major model families have emerged---conventional machine learning~\cite{liEasyPrimeMachineLearning2021,koeppelPredictionPrimeEditing2023}, deep learning~\cite{kimPredictingEfficiencyPrime2021,yuPredictionEfficienciesDiverse2023,schwankgeraldPredictingPrimeEditing2023,mathisMachineLearningPrediction2024}, and mechanistic models~\cite{hsuMechanisticMachineLearning2026}---each with distinct strengths and limitations.
Yet these models are trained and released on heterogeneous datasets, input formats, and evaluation protocols, which makes fair comparison difficult and leaves little shared infrastructure for combining complementary predictors.

To address this gap, I present a prime editing benchmarking platform that unifies large-scale experimental datasets under a common schema, evaluates state-of-the-art efficiency predictors side by side, and supports training custom models as well as ensembles that mix newly trained weights with vendor-provided ones.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Project Structure Overview}

This project consists of two main services. The first service, PE Database, can be run as a stand-alone docker application that serves data from requested data source in desirable format. The second service is PE Ensemble, which as the name suggests provides an interface for users to play with current state of the art model architectures in prime editing efficiency prediction, and relies on PE Database to provide data formatted for the selected models to train or evaluate.

\subsection{PE Database}
% overview of the database operations

With interest in prime editing guide RNA design steadily growing, the volume of big-data studies researching various aspects of prime editing guide RNA design and efficiency prediction has been increasing year by year. However, none of the studies have agreed upon a standardized format for their model inputs, and as the volume of studies involving prime editing grows, the challenge of comparing new deep learning system benchmarks becomes more pronounced. To streamline the reviewing process, I established a central database for storing prime editing experimental data. This database, called PE DB, is crafted to store and retrieve data from prime editing experiments, including guide RNA sequences, target DNA sequences, and the outcomes of editing. 

Instead of a general PE knowledge base, PE DB is tailored to function as a benchmarking dataset for prime editing efficiency prediction models that often requires a lager training set to achieve good performance. Thus the smaller datasets produced by biological studies on specific prime editing systems, such as Nelson et al.\cite{nelsonEngineeredPegRNAsImprove2022}, are not included in this project. 

However, popular validation datasets such as Anazalone et al.\cite{anzaloneSearchandreplaceGenomeEditing2019} that have direct reference in the modelling literature are included to allow more comprehensive comparison against the state of the art models, despite their small sizes.

\subsubsection{Data Source}

We pooled all of the big-data studies conducted with various versions of PEs on a number of cell lines under numerous experimental settings. Six studies are included - 

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Kim et al. 2021}
    
    Kim et al.\cite{kimPredictingEfficiencyPrime2021} conducted one of the first big-data studies on prime editing efficiency, performing a high-throughput evaluation of PE2 activity using 54,836 pairs of pegRNAs and target sequences in HEK293T cells. The study identified factors affecting PE2 efficiency and developed three computational models (including the deep learning model DeepPE) to predict pegRNA efficiency. The data are organized into four datasets in the database.

    The main library \nolinkurl{deeppe-ht} contains approximately 43,000 pegRNAs from Library~1, which focused on a G$\to$C substitution at reverse transcriptase template position +5. These pegRNAs and their corresponding synthetic target sequences were delivered via lentiviral reporters --- each construct pairs the pegRNA with an artificial target cassette rather than an endogenous genomic locus. The data are split into HT-training and HT-test subsets.

    Library~2 is split into an edit-type subset \nolinkurl{deeppe-type}, which evaluates how different mutation types (substitutions, insertions, deletions) affect PE2 efficiency, and an edit-position subset \nolinkurl{deeppe-position}, which evaluates how the position of the edit within the reverse transcriptase template affects efficiency. Both subsets use the same lentiviral reporter system in HEK293T cells with PE2. 
    % TODO: add detailed number of counts for each subset

    A separate endogenous validation dataset \nolinkurl{deeppe-endo} contains arrayed pegRNA experiments at native genomic loci in HEK293T, HCT116, and MDA-MB-231 cells, where both pegRNAs and PE2 were delivered by plasmid transfection. Editing efficiencies are averaged across biological and technical replicates (BR1--BR2 $\times$ TR1--TR2).

    \item \textbf{Yu et al. 2023}
    
    As a follow up study to Kim et al. 2021, Yu et al.\cite{yuPredictionEfficienciesDiverse2023} systematically evaluated prime editing efficiencies for 338,996 pegRNA--target pairs (including 3,979 epegRNAs) in an error-free manner across eight PE systems and seven cell types. The study developed DeepPrime, a deep learning model that predicts PE efficiencies for all possible edit types up to 3~bp, and DeepPrime-FT, a fine-tuning strategy that adapts predictions to specific PE systems and cell types.

    The main ClinVar library \nolinkurl{deepprime-clinvar} contains approximately 259,000 pegRNA--target pairs designed around ClinVar pathogenic variants. Efficiencies were measured on synthetic 74~bp target contexts via a plasmid-based assay, not at native genomic loci. Both pegRNAs and PE were delivered by plasmid transfection. This dataset spans multiple PE systems (PE2, PE2max, PE4max, NRCH-PE2, NRCH-PE2max, NRCH-PE4max, among others) and cell types (HEK293T, A549, DLD1, HeLa, MDA-MB-231, HCT116, and NIH3T3).

    The fine-tuning subsets \nolinkurl{deepprime-small} are smaller datasets used for DeepPrime-FT, covering multiple cell-line and PE-system combinations with the same synthetic target context format.

    Two off-target datasets are also stored in the database: a mismatch library \nolinkurl{deepprime-off} and its sub-pools \nolinkurl{deepprime-off-subpool}, which profile PE editing efficiencies at mismatched target sites. 

    \item \textbf{Koeppel et al. 2023}
    
    Koeppel et al.\cite{koeppelPredictionPrimeEditing2023} focused specifically on prime editing insertion efficiency, designing a library of 3,604 insertion sequences of various lengths (1--69~nt) and measuring their insertion frequencies at four endogenous genomic target sites (HEK3, EMX1, FANCF, and the safe-harbor CLYBL locus) in three human cell lines (HEK293T, HAP1, and HAP1 $\Delta$MLH1). The study identified that insertion length, nucleotide composition (particularly cytosine content), and secondary structure of the insert sequence all affect insertion rates, and discovered that the 3$'$ flap nucleases TREX1 and TREX2 suppress the insertion of longer sequences. These findings were combined into the MinsePIE machine learning model, which predicts relative insertion frequencies with $R = 0.70$.

    MinSePIE data are split into five catalog datasets by library design and delivery:
    \nolinkurl{library-insert-set12} (Set~1/2 pooled screens),
    \nolinkurl{library-insert-18nt},
    \nolinkurl{library-insert-codon-variant} (endogenous barnacle tagging),
    \nolinkurl{library-insert-codon-hek3} (codon validation at HEK3), and
    \nolinkurl{library-insert-piggybac} (Set~1 screens in HAP1 with PiggyBac-integrated PE2).
    Only 19 out of 33 sets of experimental data from the study were recorded into the database, as the experimental results for the additional validation experiments could not be located.

    \item \textbf{Schwank et al. 2023}
    
    Mathis et al.\cite{schwankgeraldPredictingPrimeEditing2023} performed a high-throughput screen analyzing prime editing outcomes of 92,423 pegRNAs targeting a highly diverse set of 13,349 human pathogenic mutations, including base substitutions, insertions, and deletions. The study identified sequence context features that influence prime editing and trained PRIDICT (PRIme editing guide preDICTion), an attention-based bidirectional recurrent neural network that predicts both editing efficiency and product purity.

    The main library \nolinkurl{library1} uses a self-targeting lentiviral reporter format in HEK293T cells: each construct pairs the pegRNA with a synthetic target cassette containing the corresponding pathogenic sequence, so edits occur at a non-endogenous reporter rather than a native chromosomal locus. The pegRNAs were delivered by lentiviral transduction and PE2 was delivered by plasmid transfection.

    The library2 dataset \nolinkurl{library2} is a disease-focused subscreen of the same lentiviral reporter format across HEK293T, U2OS, and K562 (including MLH1$^{-/-}$ lines as separate cell-line identifiers, matching PRIDICT2 naming). The library2-invivo dataset validates the same pegRNA set in GFP+ sorted mouse liver via adenoviral delivery of both pegRNAs and PE2 (endogenous liver loci).

    A separate endogenous validation dataset \nolinkurl{pridict1-endo} contains arrayed pegRNA experiments at 15 genomic loci in HEK293T and K562 (three pegRNAs per locus from library~1: the endogenous-target pegRNA, \emph{endo}, plus the highest- and lowest-PRIDICT-scoring candidates, \emph{best} and \emph{worst}). Edit types are taken from the authors' endogenous CRISPResso batch amplicons (PRIDICT supplementary files): 44 pegRNAs install a 1-bp substitution and locus~3 uses a 10-bp deletion; there are no insertion edits in this validation set.

    \item \textbf{Mathis et al. 2024}
    
    The library-diverse dataset extends the PRIDICT1 approach with a broader range of edit types, screened in HEK293T, K562, and K562 MLH1$^{-/-}$ (mismatch repair-deficient) cell lines using the same self-targeting lentiviral reporter system (non-endogenous targets). The pegRNAs were delivered by lentiviral transduction and PE2 was delivered by plasmid transfection.

    The library-diverse-invivo dataset validates a subset of the diverse-library pegRNAs at endogenous mouse liver loci in vivo, where both pegRNAs and PE were delivered via adenoviral vectors. This provides an important cross-context validation for computational predictions trained on in vitro reporter data.

    A TRIP (Thousands of Reporters Integrated in Parallel) analysis dataset profiles editing at 1,202 endogenous genomic integration sites in K562 cells, providing the chromatin context data used to train the ePRIDICT model. Every integration was edited with the same prime-editing pegRNA (\nolinkurl{TRIP\_pegRNA\_GtoC}), a uniform 1-bp G$\to$C substitution. Similarly, due to the lack of support for chromatin coordinate translation, this dataset currently has partial standardization support (entry-level fields only).

    \item \textbf{Hsu et al. 2026}
    
    Hsu et al.\cite{hsuMechanisticMachineLearning2026} developed OptiPrime, a mechanistic machine learning model that embeds prime editing biochemistry into its architecture. To train and benchmark the model, the authors performed two large paired pegRNA--target lentiviral reporter screens in HEK293T (partially mismatch-repair deficient) and HeLa (mismatch-repair proficient) cells, measuring editing with both PE2 and PE4. In both screens, pegRNA--target pairs were packaged into lentiviral libraries and transduced at low multiplicity of infection (MOI~$<0.3$); after selection for integrated library members, cells were transfected with PE construct plasmids (PEmax-Cas9 with tevoPreQ1 epegRNAs) to induce editing. Efficiencies are therefore measured on synthetic reporter cassettes rather than native chromosomal loci.

    The Lib-MMR library \nolinkurl{lib-mmr} comprises ${\sim}$9,964 pegRNAs targeting 200 randomly selected exonic sites with diverse edit types, including single and multi-base substitutions, insertions, deletions, and PAM edits. The supplementary table also lists 36 additional pegRNAs with \nolinkurl{Design category = Endogenous}; these are positive controls whose sequences were previously used to edit endogenous loci (RNF2, FANCF, HEK3, APOE, HBB, CDKL5, PRNP, and HEXA), but in this study they were assayed in the same lentiviral reporter format as the rest of Lib-MMR. PE DB therefore stores them separately as \nolinkurl{lib-mmr-controls} so that users can include or exclude reference pegRNAs without mixing them into the primary MMR diversity library.

    The Lib-CV library \nolinkurl{lib-cv} contains 10,406 pegRNAs designed to correct 944 ClinVar pathogenic variants, including up to nine silent edit combinations per variant to evade mismatch repair. Like Lib-MMR, Lib-CV uses the lentiviral pegRNA--target reporter system with PE2/PE4 plasmid transfection in HEK293T and HeLa. Across the two libraries, the authors report 74,769 editing measurements spanning 1,290 unique target sites in these four cell-line/PE-system combinations. Supplementary Table~3 (\nolinkurl{Endo\_gRNAs}) lists additional endogenous pegRNA designs used elsewhere in the paper but does not include the high-throughput efficiency measurements exported here.
    
\end{enumerate}

\autoref{fig:data_composition} summarizes the composition of data from the studies. In total, there are ${\sim}$790,000 pegRNA--target pairs from six studies. 

DeepPrime and PRIDICTv2 has good coverage of all editing types, with PRIDICTv2 having a larger range of edit length, from 1 to 15bp compared to DeepPrime's 1-3bp in the main library and up to 6bp in the fine-tuning subsets. 

Note that on top of the difference in delivery method, target context and experiment methods, the processing of editing efficiency also varies among the studies. PRIDICTv1 generously provided the raw counts of editing outcomes(successful edits, unintended edits and no edits) for each pegRNA, while other studies only provides a one-value measurement of editing efficiency. Kim et al. set the standard of using the augmented editing efficiency measurement, which deducts the background editing rate from the total editing rate, for all of their datasets\cite{kimPredictingEfficiencyPrime2021,yuPredictionEfficienciesDiverse2023}. The background editing rate is the editing rate of the pegRNA in the absence of the target sequence, measured in 0-100 scale, thus the $\div 100$ in \autoref{eq:augmented-editing-efficiency}. This measurement method is also followed by the PRIDICT studies\cite{schwankgeraldPredictingPrimeEditing2023,mathisMachineLearningPrediction2024}. Koeppel et al. however, used the ratio of successful insertions over total insertions as the editing efficiency measurement. The background editing rate was not provided in the study and thus currently impossible to recover, just bear in mind that during training of custom models this can be the source of potential discrepancy in models' performances across the datasets.

\begin{equation}
    \text{Editing efficiency} = 100 \times \frac{\text{\# successful edits} - \text{\# total read count} \times \text{background editing rate} \div 100}{\text{\# total read count} - \text{\# total read count} \times \text{background editing rate} \div 100}
    \label{eq:augmented-editing-efficiency}
\end{equation}


\subsubsection{Data Storage}

Since the project is distributed using GitHub, we needed to work around the 100 MB per file limit to avoid using Git LFS(Large File Storage) and incurring substantial charges.
Instead of a persistent DB object, the data are stored in their original tsv, csv or xlsx (Excel) format and are only transformed into standardized formats and indexed by a SQLite database after it is deployed by users.


\subsubsection{Data Formatting}

As discussed in the last section, only raw data directly acquired from the studies are stored in the repository, some preprocessing is required to convert the data into more uniformly usable format.

The first step is data exportation, where the original data was broken down into individual experiments(a 'datasheet') corresponding to one PE system operating on one cell-line with minimum processing. The original columns are respected to as much as this hierarchical design allows, pending further operations.

The exported data can be used to construct the SQLite database containing study metainformation, since the directory structures and filenames reveals detailed information required by the Datasheet table, and the Study as well as Dataset table are hardcoded for the lack of a good automated pipeline.

As briefly mentioned above, a standardized format was devised as a mediator to simply conversions between formats accepted by various models. For each model a set of two converters are devised to convert original data into standardized format and back to format used in model training and inference.

The standardization process for non-chromosomal datasets are straightforward, as they provide all necessary information in either the publication or supplementary files. Endogenous datasets such as PRIDICT2 TRIP analysis requires additional effort in translating the genomic coordinates to the target context sequence. MinsePIE on the other hand requires a number of non-conventional steps to reconstruct the sequence.

MinsePIE reports insertion efficiencies at endogenous loci, but the supplementary tables identify each target only by a short name (HEK3, EMX1, FANCF, CLYBL, and so on) together with the pegRNA spacer, PBS, and homology-arm (HA) sequences from Supplementary Table~6 (ST6). No genomic coordinates or extended wild-type context windows are provided, so the wide target-strand sequences required by the shared PE schema must be reconstructed rather than looked up from the publication.

During exportation, each MOESM5 experiment string is mapped to the corresponding ST6 pegRNA row so that spacer, PBS, HA, and annotated insertion position (\texttt{+N}) are attached to every measured insert. Where the HA encodes a non-\texttt{+1} insertion, an \texttt{-Ins-} marker splits the RTT-oriented HA into left and right segments; on the target strand these become the reverse complements of HA-right (immediately 3$'$ of the SpCas9 nick between protospacer positions 17 and 18) and HA-left (further 3$'$ of the insertion).

The genomic anchors themselves were obtained by exact (and reverse-complement) mapping of the ST6 spacers onto GRCh38/hg38, not from author-supplied coordinates. Classic PE-site coordinates cited elsewhere in the literature were checked and discarded when they did not contain the MinsePIE spacer: in particular, the HEK3 site used throughout the study maps to \textit{LINC01509} on chromosome~9 rather than the chromosome~11 locus often associated with HEK293 editing screens. For the 18-nt library, the Methods text places HEK3-S2--S6 within approximately 1~kb of that HEK3 anchor, which constrained local search when whole-genome scans were inconclusive; sites that differed from the reference by a single base (HEK3-S3/S5/S6 and some codon-variant pegRNAs) were placed by homology-arm context and the cached window was patched so that the ST6 spacer sits at a fixed 100~bp offset within a 220~bp flank. The resulting loci table (\nolinkurl{minsepie\_genomic\_loci.json}) stores chromosome, 1-based spacer start, assembly strand, and the reference window for each of the 17 ST6 targets. Standardization then inserts the measured sequence at the nick/HA-derived edit position to produce length-matched WT (N-padded at the insertion) and Mut sequences, and records the corresponding hg38 protospacer interval.


\subsubsection{Database Design}

For efficient filtering, scalable data retrieval, and improved conceptual clarity, the database is designed using a hierarchical schema based on column propagation. This approach allows shared metadata to be stored at the highest appropriate level while enabling fine-grained filtering at lower levels without data duplication.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{diagrams/illustration/database_er.png}
    \caption{ER diagram of the implemented PE-DB catalog schema (\nolinkurl{study}, \nolinkurl{dataset}, \nolinkurl{scaffold}, \nolinkurl{datasheet})}
    \label{fig:database_er}
\end{figure}

As shown in \autoref{fig:database_er}, the implemented catalog schema consists of four relational tables: \textbf{Study}, \textbf{Dataset}, \textbf{Scaffold}, and \textbf{Datasheet}.

The \textbf{Study} table represents the publication level of the hierarchy and stores one row per source study. In the current implementation it contains a unique study name, optional publication date, and optional author string. Each study is linked to one or more datasets through a one-to-many relation.

The \textbf{Dataset} table forms the intermediate layer and stores dataset-level metadata shared across many experiments. Implemented fields include dataset name, optional textual description, pegRNA delivery method, PE delivery method, edit scope, experimental method, target context, and the \nolinkurl{standardizable} flag. In this project, \nolinkurl{standardizable=True} means full conversion support to the shared standardized format, while \nolinkurl{standardizable=False} means partial support (entry-level fields only). A foreign key (\nolinkurl{study\_id}) links each dataset to its parent study.

The \textbf{Scaffold} table stores pegRNA scaffold definitions as reusable reference records. Each scaffold has an explicit identifier, scaffold name, sequence, and optional description. This separates scaffold metadata from experiment-specific metadata and allows many datasheets to reuse the same scaffold entry.

The experiment level is represented by the \textbf{Datasheet} table. Each datasheet points to one exported CSV file and stores the condition-specific metadata required by the API: file path, cell line, PE system, scaffold, number of samples, and an \nolinkurl{updated\_at} timestamp. Foreign keys (\nolinkurl{dataset\_id} and \nolinkurl{scaffold\_id}) connect each datasheet to both its parent dataset and scaffold record.

Because most metadata required for filtering and selection are fully represented within this hierarchical schema, the individual experimental data points themselves are not stored directly in the database. Instead, only file paths referencing the raw data are maintained. This design choice significantly reduces memory usage, accelerates database initialization, and allows the system to scale efficiently while deferring large data loading operations until explicitly required. When users requires to filter using fields not represented in the catalog schema, we then go through each datasheet that fulfills metadata requirements and retrieve the rows satisfying the datasheet-level filter criteria.

\subsubsection{Models}

We are only considering models with general prime editing efficiency prediction capability, so various models in DeepPE, as well as MinsePIE, who are only designed to work on specific editing types are not included in this study. Additionally, PRIDICTv1 and PRIDICTv2 has minimal changes in term of architecture, thus only the more up to date PRIDICTv2 model is studied. This leaves us with three current state of the art deep learning models - OPED\cite{liuDesignPrimeeditingGuide2023}, DeepPrime\cite{yuPredictionEfficienciesDiverse2023} and PRIDICTv2\cite{mathisMachineLearningPrediction2024}. 

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{DeepPrime}
    
    Yu et al.\cite{yuPredictionEfficienciesDiverse2023} developed DeepPrime as a hybrid convolutional and recurrent architecture that encodes inter-sequence features between the target DNA and pegRNA. One-hot target--pegRNA tensors are processed by a 2D/1D CNN stack followed by a bidirectional GRU, while 24 engineered sequence features (for example GC content, melting temperatures, and minimum folding energies) are passed through a multilayer perceptron; the two representations are concatenated to predict editing efficiency. The model was trained on the ClinVar library ($\sim$259,000 pegRNA--target pairs from 338,996 total measurements) and generalizes across substitutions, insertions, and deletions of up to 3~bp, PBS lengths of 1--17~nt, and RT lengths of 1--50~nt. On the held-out ClinVar test set ($n = 28{,}883$), DeepPrime achieved Pearson $r = 0.84$ and Spearman $R = 0.86$; performance remained high across nine edit-type subsets ($r = 0.76$--$0.89$, median $0.81$; $R = 0.70$--$0.88$, median $0.83$). The same study also introduced DeepPrime-FT, which fine-tunes the base model to 18 cell-line and PE-system combinations across seven cell types and eight PE variants.

    \item \textbf{PRIDICTv2}
    
    Mathis et al.\cite{mathisMachineLearningPrediction2024} extended the original PRIDICT framework with PRIDICT2.0, an ensemble of attention-based bidirectional recurrent neural networks. The model averages predictions from two components: one pretrained on PRIDICT library~1 and fine-tuned on the new Library-Diverse screen, and another additionally pretrained on the DeepPrime ClinVar library before fine-tuning on Library-Diverse. Dual RNN encoders with attention layers process the wild-type and edited target sequence contexts separately, enabling predictions for substitutions, insertions, and deletions of up to 15~bp in both mismatch-repair-proficient (HEK293T, K562) and mismatch-repair-deficient (K562 MLH1$^{-/-}$) contexts, as well as in vivo in mouse liver. Library-Diverse comprises 22,619 pegRNA--target pairs in HEK293T and 22,752 in K562; in fivefold cross-validation, PRIDICT2.0 outperformed PRIDICT~v1 and six other machine-learning baselines (Extended Data Fig.~2) and showed consistently higher accuracy across edit types and edit lengths (Fig.~2a--c). Whereas PRIDICT~v1 reached Spearman $R = 0.85$ for intended edits on its 92,423-pegRNA library\cite{schwankgeraldPredictingPrimeEditing2023}, PRIDICT2.0 further improved generalization to longer edits and additional cellular contexts, including validation on endogenous loci and external datasets.

    \item \textbf{OPED}
    
    Liu et al.\cite{liuDesignPrimeeditingGuide2023} developed Optimized Prime Editing Design (OPED), an interpretable nucleotide language model that applies transfer learning to pegRNA efficiency prediction. A transformer encoder extracts features from the 47-bp target sequence, while custom transformer decoders model the PBS and RT template sequences and their cross-sequence dependencies; attention weights provide post-hoc interpretability of individual nucleotide contributions. OPED was initially trained on the DeepPE high-throughput training set and validated on three held-out DeepPE subsets that were never used during training. On the HT-test, Type-test, and Position-test sets ($n = 4{,}457$, 403, and 200, respectively), OPED achieved Pearson $r = 0.769$, 0.612, and 0.628 and Spearman $R = 0.798$, 0.624, and 0.562; pegRNAs with higher predicted scores showed 8.0-, 3.1-, and 2.2-fold higher measured editing efficiencies. When retrained on the PRIDICT library~1 training split, OPED reported $r = 0.912$ and $R = 0.905$ for editing efficiency and $r = 0.810$ and $R = 0.826$ for unintended editing rates on the test set ($n = 18{,}485$), consistently outperforming DeepPE, Easy-Prime, and PRIDICT. Experimental validation with optimized pegRNAs yielded up to 44-fold higher editing efficiencies than competing design tools across PE2, PE3/PE3b, and ePE systems, and the authors released the OPEDVar database of optimized designs from over two billion candidate pegRNAs.

    \item \textbf{OptiPrime}
    
    Hsu et al.\cite{hsuMechanisticMachineLearning2026} developed OptiPrime as a mechanistic model for prime editing efficiency prediction. OptiPrime is a kinetic model that uses machine learning to predict the kinetic parameters of the prime editing reaction. 
\end{enumerate}

On top of the three deep learning models, a small number of conventional machine learning models were also selected to provide a baseline for performance comparison. 

\subsubsection{Synchronizing PyTorch Versions}

The three deep learning models originally targeted markedly different releases: DeepPrime\cite{yuPredictionEfficienciesDiverse2023} was built against PyTorch 1.10--1.11, OPED\cite{liuDesignPrimeeditingGuide2023} against PyTorch 1.6--1.8, and PRIDICTv2\cite{mathisMachineLearningPrediction2024} against PyTorch 2.0.1. This poses a challenge for running them within a shared environment.

While DeepPrime and PRIDICTv2 distribute their weights as state dictionaries and consequently load without modification under a modern PyTorch, OPED published checkpoint is a full pickled model that fails to deserialize under PyTorch~$\geq$~2.0. We therefore converted the OPED checkpoint once into a weights-only state dictionary (\nolinkurl{*\_weights.pt}) by loading the original object under a compatibility shim that maps its legacy module paths to the vendored implementation, extracting \nolinkurl{state\_dict()}, and re-serializing it. The OPED wrapper was then updated to load this portable artifact by default, to deserialize with \nolinkurl{weights\_only=True}, and to reject the legacy full-pickle checkpoints with an actionable error rather than failing silently at runtime.

Each vendor also ships multiple pretrained weight sets rather than a single checkpoint. DeepPrime provides 20 cell-line and PE-system variants (e.g.\ \nolinkurl{DeepPrime\_base}, \nolinkurl{DP\_variant\_293T\_PE4max\_Opti\_220728}), PRIDICTv2 provides 20 cross-validation runs across four experiment folders and two cell lines(HEK293T and K562, for MMR deficient and proficient cell lines), and OPED provides one portable state dictionary. The ensemble wrappers expose these as named weight sets via \nolinkurl{list\_available\_weights()}, and the evaluation API accepts an optional \nolinkurl{weights} parameter so that a specific pretrained checkpoint can be selected without retraining. When \nolinkurl{weights} is omitted, the currently loaded or freshly trained model is used instead.

\subsubsection{Preventing Data Leakage}

Model can perform exceptionally well if it is tested on examples that are too similar or even identical to the ones in the training set. This is known as data leakage, and it is a major problem in machine learning. 

In the context of prime editing efficiency prediction, data leakage can occur if the model is trained on target loci that are identical to the ones in the test set, inflating the performance metrics.

To mitigate this, PE-Ensemble performs an automatic data-leak check before running evaluation. For each selected weight set, we load the set of training target loci stored as \nolinkurl{train_target_loci.json}; each locus is represented by a universal hash identifier (\texttt{target\_uid}) computed from the wild-type protospacer sequence. We compute \texttt{target\_uid} for every row in the requested evaluation split and detect whether any test loci overlap the model's training loci. By default, overlapping evaluation is aborted (and can optionally be allowed with a warning).

For shipped vendor (pretrained) models, training provenance is reconstructed conservatively as:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{DeepPrime:} loci come from the DeepPrime author-provided ClinVar library and the fine-tuning sheets used to generate the distributed weight variants (e.g. Small and Off datasets).
    \item \textbf{PRIDICTv2 (PRIDICT2):} loci are treated as the full PRIDICT1 target loci used for PRIDICT2 training (PRIDICT1 does not provide a vendor hold-out split in PE-DB, so all target loci in the selected PRIDICT1 Library 2 cell subsets are conservatively marked as “seen”).
    \item \textbf{OPED:} loci come from DeepPE Library 1 and Library 2 (Position/Type), as represented in PE-DB standardized datasets \nolinkurl{deeppe\_ht}, \nolinkurl{deeppe\_position}, and \nolinkurl{deeppe\_type} (HEK293T; PE2 reporters).
\end{itemize}

\section{PE Database API and deployment}
\label{app:api}

\subsection{Service architecture}

PE DB is one of three cooperating services in the repository:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{PE Database} (\nolinkurl{services/pe-db}, port~8000) --- catalog metadata in SQLite and edit records on disk; owns export, standardization, filtering, and model-format conversion.
    \item \textbf{PE Ensemble} (\nolinkurl{services/pe-ensemble}, port~8001) --- vendored model wrappers, asynchronous training and evaluation jobs, and a proxy to PE Database's filter API.
    \item \textbf{PE Hub} (\nolinkurl{pe-hub}, port~5173) --- React UI for catalog browsing, export, training, and evaluation.
\end{itemize}

Shared split logic and constants live in \nolinkurl{packages/pe-common}. Vendor model code is vendored under \nolinkurl{vendor/models/} as git submodules.

\subsection{On-disk data layout}

\begin{verbatim}
datasets/
  raw/           # original study files (CSV, TSV, XLSX)
  exported/      # one CSV per datasheet (generated at startup)
  standardized/  # shared-schema parquet (generated at startup)
  formatted/     # cached model-format parquet (generated on demand)
  catalog/       # pe_database.db (SQLite; metadata only)
\end{verbatim}

Edit-level rows are never stored in SQL. The catalog records file paths and experiment metadata (\nolinkurl{cell\_line}, \nolinkurl{pe\_system}, scaffold, sample counts); measurements are read with Pandas when an endpoint is called.

\subsection{Initialization pipeline}

\nolinkurl{initialize\_database()} runs when PE Database starts (unless artifacts already exist):

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Seed} --- create SQL tables; insert Study, Dataset, and Scaffold rows from \nolinkurl{app/catalog/studies.py} and \nolinkurl{scaffolds.py}.
    \item \textbf{Export} --- write \nolinkurl{datasets/exported/} from raw files; register Datasheet rows with CSV paths.
    \item \textbf{Standardize} --- write \nolinkurl{datasets/standardized/} parquet in the shared schema.
\end{enumerate}

Administrators can re-run this pipeline with \nolinkurl{POST /api/export} or standardize a single sheet with \nolinkurl{POST /api/convert}. Environment variables \nolinkurl{PE\_DB\_FORCE\_EXPORT} and \nolinkurl{PE\_DB\_FORCE\_STANDARDIZE} force regeneration.

Supported multi-value query parameters include \nolinkurl{study}, \nolinkurl{dataset}, \nolinkurl{cell\_line}, \nolinkurl{pe\_system}, \nolinkurl{edit\_scope}, \nolinkurl{experimental\_method}, \nolinkurl{target\_context}, and \nolinkurl{scaffold\_name}.

\paragraph{Model-format export (\nolinkurl{format} set).}
Loads standardized parquet for each matching, standardizable datasheet, applies per-edit filters (\nolinkurl{edit\_type}, \nolinkurl{edit\_length}, \nolinkurl{edit\_efficiency\_min/max}), converts to the requested schema, and optionally assigns splits. \nolinkurl{split\_strategy} is required when \nolinkurl{format} is set:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \nolinkurl{none} --- no partition labels;
    \item \nolinkurl{holdout\_2} --- train/test;
    \item \nolinkurl{holdout\_3} --- train/validation/test (default 70/15/15 in PE Ensemble);
    \item \nolinkurl{cv} --- $K$-fold cross-validation, with optional held-out test partition via \nolinkurl{original\_fold}.
\end{itemize}

Splits are \textbf{group-aware}: rows sharing a \nolinkurl{group\_id} (protospacer group) stay in the same partition. Setting \nolinkurl{merge=true} concatenates multiple datasheets and reassigns \nolinkurl{group\_id} before splitting. \nolinkurl{summary\_only=true} returns counts and split summaries without row payloads.

\subsection{Integration with PyTorch training}

PE Database does not instantiate \nolinkurl{torch.utils.data.DataLoader}. The data path for training and evaluation is:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A client posts to PE Ensemble \nolinkurl{POST /train} or \nolinkurl{POST /evaluate} with dataset filters and split parameters.
    \item PE Ensemble calls \nolinkurl{GET /api/filter} on PE Database with the model's native \nolinkurl{format} and \nolinkurl{split\_strategy}.
    \item Records are assembled into a Pandas DataFrame; training excludes the test partition (\nolinkurl{exclude\_test\_partition}), evaluation selects it (\nolinkurl{select\_evaluation\_partition}).
    \item Each model wrapper encodes rows into tensors and builds a PyTorch \nolinkurl{DataLoader} (DeepPrime and OPED via PyTorch Lightning; PRIDICT2 via the vendor \nolinkurl{prepare\_data} pipeline).
\end{enumerate}

Long-running filter requests support a \nolinkurl{progress\_token} query parameter; PE Ensemble tails the corresponding log file into the job log stream. The default HTTP read timeout is 600~s (\nolinkurl{PE\_DB\_FILTER\_TIMEOUT\_SECONDS}).

\subsection{Local deployment}

From the repository root:

./start-all.sh --install   # first run: install Python and npm dependencies
